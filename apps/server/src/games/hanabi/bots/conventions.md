# Hanabi bot conventions

Use these shared conventions to interpret intent and choose actions from the supplied legal actions, visible state, and full public event history. Convention-based beliefs guide decisions but remain distinct from literal clue knowledge and proven card identities. The active game rules determine legality, playability, scoring, and when the game ends.

## Play for a perfect score

The team's objective is a perfect score in the active game mode. Protect that possibility throughout the game: be conservative about discarding needed cards and about plays made with insufficient information. Losing a life can also destroy a needed card. Judge risks by their effect on the chance of perfection, not just the expected number of points.

The group considers an attempt lost once perfection becomes impossible, such as when the only yellow 5 is discarded before being played. Automatic termination still follows the active rules and critical-discard setting. If the game remains active, continue choosing supplied legal actions; this convention does not grant an end-game or reset action.

## Interpret deliberate intent before responding

Assume all players follow these shared conventions and act deliberately, with good intentions and good insight. Following a convention, declining an expected action, and departing from a convention can each carry meaning. Develop a contextual theory of why a player chose an action before deciding how to respond, even when its immediate purpose appears obvious. Human mistakes remain possible; a departure is evidence to interpret, not proof of an emergency or a particular hidden card.

For a clue, consider who gave it, whom it targeted, which cards it touched, why that clue was chosen, and why it was given at that moment. Compare the action with alternatives supported by visible information and that player's public knowledge. A player giving a clue while apparently holding a known playable card signals that communicating something took priority over their own progress. Consider whether the timing indicates an urgent save or another obligation.

Use the board, knowledge, and positions at the time of the action. Do not assume a teammate knows the faces of their own cards just because you can see them. Do not invent hidden card identities or unavailable alternatives. Deliberate intent is the working assumption, not proof that an interpretation is correct; revise it when subsequent evidence conflicts.

## Manage clues and lives as resources

Keep track of the available clues and the clues needed to resolve upcoming problems across the table. At maximum clues, discarding is illegal; at zero clues, giving a clue is illegal and a player must play or discard. Both extremes can remove an important escape route. Keeping clues relatively high while leaving room to discard can be valuable, but there is no fixed target: spend or recover clues according to the upcoming plays, saves, and legal options.

Lives are also a resource, but a failed play normally provides neither score progress nor a replacement clue and may destroy a card needed for perfection. Preserve lives for situations that justify that cost. The emergency signal below is a costly exception, not a routine way to communicate; losing the final life ends the game and cannot enable a recovery.

## The discard queue

The densely packed top row is a discard queue. All cards begin there. Its leftmost card is the default discard; removing a queued card shifts the remaining cards left without changing their relative order, and a newly drawn card joins the rightmost end. The bottom zone holds cards set aside from that queue.

When dragging is disabled, track the same queue logically by card identity and acquisition order. A card reserved by a clue leaves the logical discard queue even though it remains in its physical slot; the default discard becomes the oldest unreserved queued card. Interpret teammates' hands the same way. Physical position alone does not make a reserved card discardable. Never request a rearrangement when the game disallows dragging.

Treat discarding the leftmost logically queued card as the default action on every player's turn unless they have a known or convention-signaled play, a clearly established urgent obligation, or a legal restriction that prevents discarding. Discarding another card requires a specific exceptional reason; do not plan around that rare departure. A player without an established action should assume their default discard is safe. The team cannot clue every card, so the absence of a warning supports this assumption; it is a shared convention, not proof of the card's identity or safety.

When predicting a teammate's turn, do not assume they will give a clue merely because they have clue tokens or could find a useful clue. If they have no established play or recognizable obligation to act otherwise, they may discard. Judge their available plays and obligations from their own information, not from the card faces you can see. A speculative future clue is not protection for their discard slot.

If a teammate's default discard is critical to a perfect score and they have no established action that keeps it safe, treat preventing that discard as an urgent obligation. Find a supported way to protect it before the threatened turn: give a clue that causes the card to be set aside, establish a play of another card, or coordinate an obligation the teammate can recognize. In an extreme emergency, the failed-play signal below can justify spending a life on another card to preserve the critical one, provided the sacrificed card and lost life do not themselves make perfection impossible. A play that buys a turn only postpones the threat; plan the follow-up save and reassess the queue after each action. Do not leave the critical card exposed on the hope that its holder will improvise a clue or discard elsewhere.

## Good touches preserve independently useful cards

In the vast majority of cases, every card touched by a clue is a good touch: each is still needed and is either playable now or can become playable later. This applies to all clues, including multi-card clues. The recipient should assume the giver intends every touched card to contribute independently, and normally move all of them out of the discard queue and into the bottom zone. A clue touching several 2s asks you to preserve each of them; use the board and the play conventions below to decide which to play now.

As the clue giver, normally choose clues that touch only useful cards. Avoid clues that cause someone to preserve already-useless cards or redundant copies. Two cards with the same color and rank cannot both contribute: usually wait for the recipient to safely discard one copy, then clue the remaining copy. An urgent or exceptional situation can justify a clue that also touches a useless or discardable card, but it should have a clear contextual purpose that outweighs the misleading good-touch expectation.

As the recipient of a multi-card clue, normally infer that its touched cards are not duplicate identities. For example, on an empty board with ordinary ascending fireworks, a clue touching two 1s means both are immediately playable, and the good-touch convention means you should assume they are different colors. Their rank and the board establish initial playability; the convention supports playing both without expecting the second to duplicate the first. Recheck the board before each play. This expectation of distinct identities is a convention-based inference, not a new literal clue constraint. Use the active mode's build rules when deciding which ranks are playable.

A rare exception is when you can infer that a touched card is safe to discard. You may leave it in the queue or move it to the leftmost position. Preserve useful placements and relative order elsewhere. Placement below the queue communicates retention, not a confirmed identity or guaranteed playability.

Rearrange cards only on your own turn, immediately after receiving a clue, or in the follow-up after your own play or discard. Movement is optional and consumes no turn. After an off-turn clue, choose a layout or leave it unchanged without taking a gameplay action. If the clue also makes it your turn, combine your desired layout with that turn's action. When dragging is disabled, preserve reservations logically and do not request movements.

After your own play or discard, briefly interpret the revealed card and outcome and adjust reservations or the permitted layout if needed. This includes successful plays, failed plays, and discards, including an action that ends the game. Keep this follow-up concise and focused on the result; it is not another full turn of planning and grants no second gameplay action. Giving a clue does not receive this result follow-up.

## Single-card play signals

A clue touching exactly one card signals a play if at least one identity consistent with the recipient's known information could be played on the board at the time of the clue. In that case, assume the giver intends the card to be playable and use the playable possibilities to interpret its identity.

For example, with ordinary ascending fireworks, if red 1 is the only card played and a rank-2 clue touches exactly one card, infer that it is the playable red 2. The clue proves the rank; the convention supplies the inferred color and play intent. If no consistent identity is playable, the play signal does not apply. At an empty ordinary board, a single-card rank-4 clue is a retention clue, not a play signal. Always evaluate playability using the active mode's rules, including any different build order.

This single-card play signal applies only when exactly one card is touched. A multi-card clue follows the newest-card convention below when it touches the recipient's newest card. Playability can also follow from the ranks, board, and good-touch interpretation, as with two different-color 1s at the start of an ordinary game. Otherwise preserve the touched cards and use additional evidence to decide when to play them.

## Play a playable multi-card group newest first

When a clue touches multiple cards and all of those cards are believed playable, play that group from most recently acquired to least recently acquired: right to left in the original, unreordered discard queue. This order applies to the cards touched by that clue, not to replacement cards drawn later. Play one card per turn and recheck playability against the current board before each play.

Determine card age from the recorded draw history and original deal order, using the rightmost initially dealt card as newest among that deal. Moving cards between zones or reordering them does not change their age. Interpret the clue using the cards and their acquisition order at clue time.

## Multi-card clues that touch the newest card

If a clue touches multiple cards and includes the recipient's most recently acquired card still in hand at clue time, the recipient should assume that newest card is playable. It must be the newest card in the whole hand, not merely the newest among the touched cards. The play signal stays attached to that card even after rearrangement or a later draw. Plan to play it on your turn; preserve the other touched cards under the good-touch convention unless there is separate evidence that they too are playable. When all touched cards are believed playable, follow newest-first play order.

This lets one clue supply an immediate play, protect other useful cards, and convey information about all of them. As the giver, check the actual face of the recipient's newest card against the current board and active build rules before choosing any multi-card clue that touches it. If that card is not playable, normally do not give that clue: the recipient will interpret it as a play signal, even if your intended purpose was only to save older cards. The specific exceptions are a supported finesse that makes it playable before the recipient acts, or the costly failed-play emergency signal described below. Information gain and good touches alone do not justify either exception.

Timing can make an otherwise useful clue safe later. If the recipient can safely discard and draw, waiting may let you clue the same older cards without touching their new newest card. If discarding would lose something important, help them make a safe play of another card so they can draw before you give the intended clue. Re-evaluate the complete touched set, newest card, and board after that draw; the replacement may also match the clue, and an empty deck provides no new card. Do not sacrifice a needed card just to change which card is newest.

The recipient's inferred playability remains a convention-based belief, not literal knowledge. Check it against proven constraints and the board before acting; receiving this clue off-turn still permits only rearrangement until your turn.

## Protect twos early in ordinary games

In games without Black Powder, give high priority to saving a needed 2 that is approaching the discard slot, even when its 1 is not yet available. Treat that save as close in urgency to protecting a last remaining copy, but not as an absolute rule: discarding the first copy of a 2 does not by itself make perfection impossible. Consider the visible copies, existing reservations, clue supply, and competing obligations. This opening strategy does not apply unchanged to either Black Powder mode.

Each ordinary 2 has only two copies. If the first is discarded, the remaining copy may arrive near the end, leaving too few turns to build the rest of its suit. While waiting for that 2, players may also discard 3s and 4s, making their remaining copies critical and creating more saves and awkward late-game dependencies. Preserving an early 2 keeps the suit easier to build and reduces that pressure.

The opening offers a useful window for retention clues. Before any 1 is played on an ordinary empty board, a rank-2 clue touching exactly one card cannot imply an immediately playable identity, so it safely asks the recipient to reserve that card. This can justify saving a threatened 2 before clueing a playable 1. Once a 1 is on the board, a single-card rank-2 clue becomes a play signal whenever a playable 2 remains consistent with the recipient's knowledge. For example, with only red 1 built, such a clue can imply red 2; do not use it as a routine save for blue 2 when that would mislead the recipient. Evaluate the clue against the board and knowledge at the time it is given.

Opening clues commonly identify playable 1s, protect threatened 2s, or save single-copy 5s. A rank-2 clue can preserve several independently useful 2s, but it must still respect the newest-card play convention: early protection does not create an exception for a multi-card clue that misleadingly signals a play on an unplayable newest card. Apply good-touch expectations and avoid reserving redundant copies.

Unprotected 3s and 4s are usually acceptable early discards while another copy remains available. Avoid setting them aside unnecessarily: reserved cards occupy limited hand space and reduce room to draw and cycle cards. They are not useless cards, and an urgent playable card or last remaining needed copy still deserves protection. As the board and discard pile change, reassess these opening priorities rather than preserving a fixed rank preference throughout the game.

## Leave a warning window after two-player discards

In a two-player game, give a modest extra preference to a useful clue or a normally supported safe play after your partner discards a still-needed 3 or 4. Consecutive discards can remove both copies before either player has a chance to warn the other. This deserves particular attention for ordinary 3s and 4s because they are often left in the discard queue, while 1s are commonly clued for play and threatened 2s and 5s are commonly protected.

For example, both players may have an unclued blue 3 as their default discard. Your partner discards theirs and discovers its identity only after committing the action. They can now see that your blue 3 is the last needed copy, but cannot give a clue before your turn. If you immediately discard too, perfection is lost. Their failure to warn you before their discard is not reassurance: they did not yet know that discarding their own card would make yours critical. Taking a useful non-discard action gives them another turn to warn you; account for whether they will have a clue available to do so.

The chance of matching discards in any one instance may be small, but repeated exposure makes this worth a slight adjustment in priorities. It is not a ban on consecutive discards, a reason to assume your discard matches, or a request to reserve every unknown card. The concern does not apply when that identity is already built, your discard candidate cannot match it, or the remaining needed copy is otherwise accounted for safely. Respect clue meanings and more urgent obligations; do not replace a reasonable discard with a misleading clue or unsafe play merely to avoid discarding twice in a row.

## Give clues that create useful action

Normally spend a clue to enable a play or protect an at-risk needed card, including the high-priority saves described above. More knowledge alone does not justify spending a clue and a turn. If a recipient's cards are already safely set aside, a clue that refines their identities without enabling a play or preventing a needed discard usually accomplishes too little, especially when the recipient already has a safe discard available.

Defer that extra information until it can also make a card playable or address a real threat. Later board progress, draws, or clues may create a better opportunity to communicate it while advancing the game. Prefer a useful play, save, or safe discard over an information-only clue. Such a clue is a last resort when no productive alternative is available; account for legal restrictions such as being unable to discard at maximum clues. Judge usefulness across the table: a supported finesse or a clue that keeps a player occupied to protect an endangered card has an actionable purpose even if the recipient cannot play immediately.

## Prefer clues that add new information

Clue tokens are scarce. When urgency, safety, and convention meaning are otherwise comparable, prefer the clue that adds the most useful new information across the recipient's entire hand. Consider both positive information on touched cards and negative information on every untouched card, using the active mode's clue-matching rules. Compare how each clue narrows the recipient's remaining possible identities, not just how many cards it touches or how many clue marks it adds.

Use the recipient's existing knowledge for each current card. Repeating an already-known fact or exclusion adds no literal information. Knowledge stays with the card that received it; a replacement draw does not inherit an earlier card's exclusions.

For example, suppose a yellow 1 is the recipient's only 1 and the only card matched by a yellow clue. Both clues touch the same card. If several other cards are already known not to be 1s because they were present for an earlier rank-1 clue, another rank-1 clue repeats those exclusions. A yellow clue can add fresh not-yellow information to those cards. Prefer yellow when its overall information gain is greater and it serves the same strategic purpose.

Information gain is a tie-breaker, not a reason to give misleading play signals, make bad touches, or delay an urgent save. A repeated clue can still communicate useful intent through timing or conventions even when it adds little literal knowledge.

## Coordinate several turns ahead

In three-, four-, and five-player games, consider each player's upcoming obligations and legal options in seating order. Normally leave a later player's problem to the teammate who acts immediately before them when that teammate can see the problem and has a suitable response. Act earlier when that response would be unavailable, insufficient, or displaced by another urgent obligation. Budget the clues for the whole sequence, not only your own action.

Deliberate inaction can coordinate several players. Suppose the next two players both have endangered discards, and your single clue cannot resolve both problems. If you can safely discard, the next player may be obliged to clue the following player, who then has a convention-signaled play. Your discard recovers a clue, and their clue and play occupy both turns without either endangered discard occurring. Use this sequence only when the next player has enough information to recognize the obligation, can give the needed clue, and the following player can make the intended play. Leaving a problem unresolved without such a supported response is not coordination.

The same reasoning can extend across four or five seats: one action can establish obligations for the rest of the table. Treat the sequence as a prediction, not control over the other players. Teammates see your hand and may optimally choose a different action because of information you lack. Reassess after each action and interpret a departure before treating it as a mistake.

## Finesses connect plays through another player

A finesse uses one clue to induce a connecting play by an intervening player, making the clued card playable before its recipient acts. Use it only when the seating order, visible cards, existing knowledge, and active build rules support that sequence.

For example, on an empty ordinary ascending board, the next player has blue 1 as their newest card, and the following player has blue 2 as their newest card. Give the following player a blue clue that signals a play under the single-card or newest-card convention. The recipient interprets the clue as a playable blue card. The intervening player sees the blue 2 and recognizes that it needs blue 1 first. They infer that they are expected to play the connecting blue 1 from their own hand before the recipient plays blue 2. Thus one clue can produce two plays.

The connecting player first considers already-clued cards whose known information could fit the required connector. An already-clued plausible connector takes priority over blindly playing the newest card. If there is no such card, the convention points to their newest card at the time of the finesse clue, provided its known information does not rule out the connector. A later draw does not inherit that signal. If several clued cards fit, this convention supplies no arbitrary tie-breaker: use the history and context, and do not treat an unresolved choice as a certain identity.

As the giver, verify that the expected connecting card actually supplies the missing step and that the intervening player has a reasonable basis to recognize the finesse. As the recipient, recheck the board before playing; if the connecting play does not happen, reassess the signal. A finesse does not authorize a play that contradicts proven card information.

## Recognize emergency signals after the outcome

A clue giver may deliberately trigger the normal play convention on an actually unplayable card when no single ordinary clue can resolve all the urgent problems. The recipient follows the normal signal and plays. Only when the card is revealed, fails, and costs a life does the recipient discover the emergency message; they are not expected to recognize this special meaning in advance or knowingly sacrifice a life themselves.

After that failed play, reconsider what the giver could see and why normal clues were insufficient. Often both the default discard and the next card in the discard queue need protection, but one clue could not safely save both. Set aside the endangered cards, physically or logically according to the dragging setting, and record the interpretation. Identify the relevant queue at the time of the clue; a replacement draw and subsequent movement do not change which cards were threatened. This is a contextual inference, not a rule that every failed play proves those two cards are valuable. Mistakes and other explanations remain possible.

Deliberate departures from expected discards also call for caution. A player who discards a card they were conventionally told to play, or discards a card other than the default queued card, may be signaling that the table's assumptions are badly wrong. Reassess outstanding play signals and reservations, proceed conservatively, and use visible evidence to work out what needs protection. The anomalous discard alone does not establish a particular hidden identity or a fixed set of cards to save.
