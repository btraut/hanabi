# Hanabi bot conventions

These are the team's shared conventions for choosing actions and interpreting intent. Assume every player understands and follows them. Use the visible state, received clues, and public history; judge a partner's choices from what they know about their own hand. Convention-based beliefs guide play but do not change literal clue constraints or the active game rules. The examples below use ordinary fireworks that build from 1 through 5; apply the active rules when playing other modes.

## Protect the game and advance the board

Value not losing above everything else. Preserve the possibility of completing every required firework. A **critical tile** is a still-needed tile whose loss would make that completion impossible, such as the last remaining copy of a needed rank. Avoid unnecessary risks with tiles and lives. A gamble can be justified when failure has little consequence relative to the remaining resources or when no good alternative exists. Deliberate sacrifices to send emergency signals belong to desperate situations where ordinary actions cannot protect the game.

On most turns, choose actions in this order:

1. Prevent a partner from imminently discarding a playable or critical tile by giving them something else to do: save that tile, enable another play, or rely on a clue obligation they can recognize and fulfill.
2. Play a tile you know or conventionally believe is playable.
3. Give a clue that lets another player play a tile.
4. Discard from your discard slot.

This order is a shared expectation. A player with no play or productive clue will normally discard. If their discard would lose an important tile, you are responsible for preventing it. Do not assume they can identify a danger in their own hidden hand. In return, trust your partners to protect your critical tiles: when you have no play, useful clue, or recognizable urgent obligation, assume your discard slot is safe.

If you have a known or convention-signaled playable tile, partners expect you to play it or give a useful clue. Their silence does **not** mean you can safely discard in that situation. Having a play removes the normal reassurance that discarding is safe, including discarding a different tile from your queue.

## The discard queue and discard slot

The ordered top row is the **discard queue**. Its leftmost tile is the **discard slot**, the tile to discard when there is nothing better to do. Removing a tile closes the gap while preserving the remaining order. New draws enter at the right. The rightmost queued tile is therefore usually the most recent draw, but these conventions refer to its queue position, not the newest tile anywhere in the hand.

Set useful clued tiles aside in the lower area, outside the queue. Preserve their relative left-to-right order so that play order remains understandable. Interpret clues using the queue and board at the time of the clue, before moving the touched tiles. A play signal stays with its tile after rearrangement or a later draw.

When dragging is disabled, maintain the same queue logically: track set-aside tiles by their stable IDs, exclude them from the queue, and preserve the order of the remaining tiles. Do the same when interpreting partners' hands. Only rearrange at the opportunities permitted by the player contract.

## Good touches mean keep the tiles

Assume every tile touched by a clue is useful now or eventually. Unless you can verify otherwise, assume it neither duplicates a tile already played nor duplicates another tile already touched by a clue. Preserve all touched tiles, including those not yet playable. A retention clue can remove a threatened tile from the discard slot without giving its holder an immediate play.

As the giver, prefer clues whose entire touched set satisfies this expectation. Consider tiles already reserved throughout the table, including what your own clues suggest about your hidden hand. For example, if your own tiles have been clued yellow, avoid also clueing a partner's yellow tiles unless you can establish that they do not duplicate yours. Hidden information can make accidental bad touches unavoidable; this is a preference to apply intelligently, not a requirement to prove every unknown identity before giving a clue.

A verified useless or redundant tile can be discarded. Do not discard a reserved tile merely because it might be a duplicate. A deliberate clue that includes duplicates requires a plan to resolve the duplication before it causes a failed play; the right-to-left convention below can make this possible.

## Clues that signal a play

### A clue touching exactly one tile

If a clue touches exactly one tile and at least one identity consistent with the recipient's literal information would be playable on the board at clue time, assume that tile is playable. Narrow its inferred identity to the playable possibilities. For example, if only red has a played 2 and a single-tile 3 clue could represent red 3, infer that the tile is red 3.

If no consistent identity is playable, the clue asks for retention. On an empty ordinary board, a single-tile 2 clue safely asks the recipient to save the 2. Once a 1 has been played, the same clue can signal a play and must be evaluated accordingly.

A play signal does not force an immediate play when a more urgent save or useful clue takes priority. It does mean you should preserve that tile and should not assume that discarding another tile is safe.

### A multi-tile clue touching the rightmost queued tile

If a clue touches multiple tiles and includes the rightmost tile in the recipient's discard queue at clue time, assume that rightmost queued tile is playable. Preserve every other touched tile under good touch; they are not automatically playable too.

The signal targets the rightmost tile in the queue, even if a more recently acquired tile is already set aside. It does not target whichever touched tile happens to be farthest right when the actual rightmost queued tile is untouched. Evaluate the trigger before setting the newly clued tiles aside.

### Respect what your clue will make people do

Before giving a clue, work out its meaning to the recipient. You cannot use a play-signaling clue as an ordinary save for a tile that is not playable. The exceptions are a supported finesse that makes it playable before their turn and a deliberate emergency signal described below. Good intentions do not erase a misleading play signal.

Waiting for a draw can make a misleading multi-tile clue safe. If the recipient plays or safely discards and draws, their rightmost queued tile changes; a later clue may then preserve the older tiles without signaling an unwanted play. Recheck the entire touched set and the rightmost queued tile after the draw, because the replacement may also match. An empty deck provides no replacement. Never sacrifice an important tile or delay an urgent save just to change the clue's meaning.

Clue tokens are a resource meant to advance the game. Spend them to create plays and prevent important losses. Clues that neither save an endangered tile nor give an immediate play are usually poor uses of a turn; early protection of 2s and preserving the endgame's remaining draws are valuable exceptions. Do not hoard clues while productive plays can be enabled, but look ahead so the team can still afford urgent saves. At zero clues you cannot clue; at the clue limit you cannot discard. Choose among the supplied legal actions.

### Use information gain to break ties

When clues have comparable urgency, safety, and useful effect, prefer the one that adds more information across the recipient's whole hand. Count both what touched tiles are and what untouched tiles cannot be. For example, if a yellow clue and a 1 clue identify the same playable yellow 1, prefer yellow when the other tiles are already known not to be 1s but have no yellow exclusions. Use each current tile's existing knowledge; replacement draws do not inherit earlier clues or exclusions.

Information gain is only a tie-breaker. It does not justify a misleading play signal, a bad touch, an information-only clue over a productive action, or delaying an urgent save. A repeated clue can still be useful when its timing communicates a needed action.

## Advanced advice

### Keep enough tiles in the discard queue

Be conservative about how many tiles you get a player to set aside. A short discard queue gives the team less time to identify and save critical tiles before they reach its left end. If a tile is neither critical nor immediately playable, usually leave it unclued in the queue. Early protection of 2s remains a useful exception. This guides which clues to give; recipients should still preserve tiles already touched under good touch.

If a clue touches your discard-slot tile but does not give you enough information to play anything, suspect that tile is critical. A playable tile would usually have received a play clue earlier, and a non-critical tile could usually have been allowed to go. Treat this as a reason to preserve the tile, not proof of its exact identity or remaining copy count; opening saves of 2s and other contextual purposes can explain the clue too.

### Budget clues, draws, lives, and final turns

Consider the remaining resources throughout the game. The opening has plentiful clues, undealt tiles, and lives. For much of the game, balance spending clues to advance play against discarding to replenish them. As the deck shrinks, compare every prospective discard with the plays still needed to finish. Even discarding a useless tile can lose the game by consuming a draw the team needed for a scoring play.

When more tiles need to be played than remain in the deck, treat that as a warning that discarding may no longer be affordable. Include the final turns after the last draw, the order in which needed tiles can be played, and which players will get those turns; deck size alone does not establish the exact limit. Once the remaining draws and final turns are needed for completion, stop discarding unless doing so is necessary to preserve a viable route to finish.

A seemingly useless clue can be the correct action when it lets another player act without consuming a draw and avoids a losing discard. Prefer a useful clue when available, respect the clue's play signals, and account for the clue tokens needed afterward. Trust that your discard-slot tile is expendable does not establish that the team can afford the draw. Once the deck is empty, a clue also consumes one of the final turns: use the supplied remainingTurns countdown and spend those turns on the sequence that can complete the game.

### Advance low ranks and protect early 2s

Ordinary suits need their low ranks before higher ranks can contribute. Advance 1s and 2s quickly: reserved tiles occupy limited hand space, and stalled suits force the team to hold high ranks for too long.

Protect early 2s especially carefully. Each ordinary 1 has three copies, while each 2 has only two. Losing an early 2 can leave a suit waiting until late in the deck and make its higher ranks much harder to preserve and play. This makes an opening clue touching one or more 2s a common useful choice, even before they can be played.

An opening single-tile 2 clue is a safe retention clue because no 2 is plausibly playable yet. An opening multi-tile 2 clue must still respect the rightmost-queue play signal: do not touch an unplayable rightmost queued 2 unless a supported finesse or emergency justifies that signal. Protecting 2s does not override preventing the loss of a critical tile. Apply this opening advice to ascending suits; different build orders require different priorities.

Unprotected 3s and 4s are usually reasonable early discards while another copy remains available. Avoid setting them aside without a useful purpose: they occupy space needed to draw and cycle tiles. This does not make them useless or override protecting an imminent playable or critical discard. Reassess as the board and discard pile change.

## Steer the next player and look beyond them

Your greatest responsibility is usually the next player in turn order. Estimate their likely play, clue, or discard, then look through the remaining players for problems and possible solutions. Budget enough clues for that sequence. You cannot predict everything because partners can see your hidden tiles, but you should be able to identify a plausible response to each imminent critical threat.

You do not always need to clue a threatened player yourself. A partner can save their own discard slot by spending their turn helping someone else. For example, A, B, and C act in that order, and both B and C have critical tiles in their discard slots. A may leave both unclued if B can see C's danger and can give C a safe save or play clue. B then clues C instead of discarding, protecting B's tile for that turn while addressing C's danger.

This requires a recognizable obligation and a suitable clue that B can afford when their turn arrives. It does not require A to prearrange the clue explicitly. A must still take a legal action, and must not discard merely to recover a clue when A's own known play makes discarding unsafe. Giving someone another play only buys time for their threatened tile; revisit the danger before they run out of actions.

### Leave a warning window after a two-player discard

In a two-player game, a partner's discard can make a matching tile in your own discard slot critical before they get a chance to warn you. For example, your partner discards a needed blue 3 and only then discovers its identity. If your discard slot also holds blue 3, immediately discarding it loses the last copy. Their silence before their discard is not reassurance about a danger that the discard itself created.

After a partner discards a still-needed 3 or 4, give a slight extra preference to a useful clue or supported safe play that lets them take another turn and warn you if necessary. Check that they will have a clue available. This is a narrow caution, not a ban on consecutive discards or a reason to reserve every unknown tile. It does not apply when your discard cannot match, the identity is already built, or the remaining copy is safely accounted for elsewhere. Do not give a misleading clue or make an unsafe play just to create a warning window.

## Play multiple playable tiles from right to left

When you have multiple known or conventionally playable tiles, play them from right to left unless there is a specific reason to choose another order, such as a required finesse connector. This applies across your playable tiles, not only those touched by one clue. Preserve the relative order of set-aside tiles so partners can predict which will be played next. Reassess playability after each action.

Predictable order lets the team handle some imperfect touches. For example, a starting hand containing two red 1s, a blue 1, and a yellow 1 can receive a 1 clue even though it includes a duplicate. The giver must use the expected right-to-left sequence to arrange a follow-up that resolves the redundant red 1 before it is played. Do not expect the recipient to recognize the duplicate without information. Only use this approach when the timing leaves an actual opportunity to address it.

## Finesses connect several players with one clue

A finesse gives a downstream player a clue that signals a playable tile even though it needs a connecting play first. An intervening player sees the clued tile and realizes they are responsible for making it playable before the recipient acts. One clue can therefore produce multiple plays.

For example, A gives a single-tile red clue to C's red 2 while red 1 is unplayed. C interprets the clue as a playable red tile. B sees that red 2 needs red 1 and infers that A is asking B to play red 1 first.

If you identify yourself as the connecting player, first use a tile already set aside whose known information fits the needed connector. Choose the rightmost matching candidate. In the example, play the rightmost set-aside 1 if it can be red 1, or the rightmost set-aside red tile if it can be red 1. If several candidates across those groups fit, use the rightmost compatible candidate. If none fits, play the rightmost tile in your discard queue at the time of the finesse clue, provided its literal information does not rule out the connector. A replacement draw does not inherit the signal.

As the giver, check that this convention selects the actual needed connector and that the intervening player can recognize their responsibility from the visible sequence. In larger games, establish which intervening player supplies each missing step; do not assume that several ambiguous players will spontaneously choose the right chain. Finesses are highly valuable when the sequence works. As the recipient, check that the connector happened before playing; if it did not, reinterpret the situation instead of blindly following a stale play signal.

## Handle desperate situations deliberately

Difficult games sometimes require actions outside the normal conventions. Assume players have a reason for a surprising action and try to understand why it was their best available option. Look for critical discard threats, exhausted clues, misleading saves, and competing obligations. Interpret the action in context rather than automatically dismissing it as a mistake.

### Spend a life to send an emergency warning

Lives are a resource, but losing one is an inefficient way to communicate. A failed play advances no score, restores no clue, and consumes a replacement from the deck if one remains. With an empty deck, it consumes a final turn instead. Weigh that entire cost against the available clues, lives, draws, and plays still needed. A low-consequence gamble or a risk taken because no good alternative exists can be reasonable, but a spare life alone does not make the failure affordable.

When ordinary actions cannot protect the game, you may deliberately give a clue that falsely signals a play on an actually unplayable, non-critical tile. The recipient follows the play signal, loses a life, and learns that the giver needed to communicate an emergency. The cost must leave the team able to continue: do not sacrifice a critical tile or the final life.

After such a failed play, reconsider the giver's view of your discard queue at clue time. The warning suggests that one or more tiles at its left end need protection. Set aside the threatened tiles you can identify from that context and play conservatively until further information resolves the danger. Do not automatically assign identities or always save exactly two tiles. The signal becomes apparent through the failed play; the recipient need not recognize the lie beforehand.

### Break the expected action when normal saves fail

If a critical tile must be saved but there are no clues, no one clue can prevent all the imminent losses, or every available clue would cause a critical tile to be played or discarded, do not passively accept the loss. An unusual play or discard can signal that partners must reconsider their queues and act conservatively. This can include playing or discarding a tile other than the discard-slot tile.

Choose a departure that gives the team a chance to recover without itself ending that chance. Partners should interpret the unexpected action as a reason to reassess threats and reservations, not as proof of a specific hidden identity. Prefer an ordinary save, supported play, or coordinated clue whenever one can solve the problem; emergency signals are the fallback when those options fail.
